Overview

About the Dissertation

Ministerial advisers occupy a critical but largely invisible position at the heart of executive power. Sitting between the political minister and the civil service, they shape the advice that informs government decisions — yet we know surprisingly little about who they are, how they are recruited, and where they end up after leaving office.

This dissertation addresses these gaps by studying the full career trajectories of 1,521 ministerial advisers who served in Belgian ministerial cabinets between 1999 and 2020. Rather than relying on surveys or interviews, it takes a text-as-data approach — building a novel software pipeline that collects, structures, and analyses career data from online sources at scale, and applies NLP methods to study adviser behaviour in the media.

Central Research Question

"To what extent can Belgian ministerial cabinets be characterised as partitocratic or technocratic?"

The dissertation approaches this question through three lenses — recruitment, roles, and post-cabinet careers — building towards a coherent picture of how ministerial cabinets function and what they reveal about the relationship between politics and expertise in Belgian governance.

Structure

The Four Studies

Study 1

A Career Dataset of Flemish Ministerial Advisors

Building a novel dataset of 754 ministerial advisers and 5,828 career positions from online sources. Introduces the software pipeline — combining web scraping, BERT-based coding assistance, and manual validation — that underpins the rest of the dissertation.

Dataset Web Scraping BERT Career Data
Study 2

Insiders, Outsiders, and Ideology: Explaining Recruitment Patterns

Who gets recruited into ministerial cabinets — political insiders or technical experts? This study shows that both models coexist, and that the balance between them is shaped by a party's ideological position and its access to political resources.

Recruitment Ideology Multilevel Models Partitocracy
Study 3

Between Politics and Policy: Partisanship and Expertise in Action

Going beyond career backgrounds to ask whether advisers actually behave in partisan ways. Using a fine-tuned language model applied to a large media corpus, this study finds that 39% of advisers appear in partisan contexts — and that career background is a significant, if modest, predictor.

NLP Media Analysis BERT Partisanship
Study 4

The Ministerial Cabinet as a Stepping Stone?

Does serving as a ministerial adviser lead to better career outcomes? This study develops a novel LLM-based algorithm that ranks the prestige of post-cabinet positions, finding that cabinet service does function as a stepping stone — though the degree to which depends heavily on party affiliation.

Results

Key Findings

45%

of Flemish ministerial advisers held a partisan position prior to entering the cabinet — suggesting the partitocratic model holds, but far from exclusively.

39%

of advisers appeared in partisan contexts in media coverage — a minimum estimate that closely mirrors the career-based partisanship figure, validating the approach.

Both

the partitocratic and technocratic models hold simultaneously in Belgium — recruitment and behaviour reflect an ongoing tension between political loyalty and administrative expertise.
